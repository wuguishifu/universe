# H16: time-series momentum across many markets (Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen, 2012)

Pre-registration written 2026-11-15, before any return of any of the ETFs below was computed. What I have looked at so far: file existence, bar counts, first/last dates, and the median daily dollar volume over the last 252 bars (a liquidity number, not a return) for a list of candidate ETFs, plus Alpaca's current `shortable`/`easy_to_borrow` flags. Part 2 (LOG.md) has already used 2017–2026 for SPY, EFA, EEM, IWM, TLT, IEF, GLD, DBC, VNQ; the other ETFs here have never been used in this project.

## Hypothesis

Moskowitz, Ooi and Pedersen (2012, JFE, "Time series momentum") and Hurst, Ooi and Pedersen (2017, "A century of evidence on trend-following investing") find that an asset's own past 12-month excess return predicts the sign of its next month's excess return, across equity indices, bonds, commodities and currencies. A portfolio long the assets with positive 12-month excess returns and short the ones with negative returns, each scaled to the same ex-ante volatility, earns a positive return with a Sharpe near 1 before 2009 (≈0.7 net over a century), is nearly uncorrelated with equities, and does best in prolonged crises. The gain comes from diversification across many uncorrelated markets.

**Prediction:** over 2017-03 → 2026-11, a volatility-scaled TSMOM portfolio over ~26 ETFs, one per market, has a higher Sharpe and a smaller max drawdown than the same ETFs held always long at equal risk, and is positive in 2022.

**Who's on the other side:** in the published account, investors who under-react to news at first (slow-moving capital, anchoring, central banks smoothing rates, commodity producers and hedgers paying to lay off risk) and later over-react; and hedgers whose demand for insurance makes trend followers' positions profitable on average. The known critique: trend following had a poor 2009–2019 (low Sharpe, choppy markets, frequent reversals), and AQR's own live results in the 2010s were far below the backtests. 2017–2026 is after both papers, so this window is out of sample for the published rule.

## Data and timing

- `data/daily/<T>.json` (adjusted for splits and dividends; open = opening print, close = official close, per Part 2's check). Months = 21 trading days, so 12 months = 252 trading days, as in Part 2.
- **Decision** at the close of the last trading day of each calendar month; **trade** at the next trading day's open, fractional shares. Weights drift between rebalances (shares are held).
- **Evaluation window:** 2017-03-01 → 2026-11-13, starting from cash at the open of 2017-03-01 (first decision on the 2017-02-28 close), the same window as Part 2, so SPY, 60/40 and the daily statistics can be checked against Part 2's numbers.
- Daily returns use adjusted close-to-close; on a rebalance day, close-to-open with the old holdings, then open-to-close with the new.
- Data check before results: every ETF has a bar on each of SPY's dates (a missing bar carries the last close forward, reported); daily moves beyond ±25% are listed so a bad split adjustment can be spotted.

## Rule (primary, two co-primaries ★)

For each ETF i at decision date t:

- **Signal:** s_i = sign(R12_i − R12_BIL), where R12 = close_t / close_{t−252} − 1 on adjusted closes (total return, so the excess is over BIL's total return over the same 252 days). (MOP: sign of the past 12-month excess return.)
- **Volatility:** σ_i = annualised standard deviation of the last 63 daily simple returns (×√252). Covariance Σ = the 63-day sample covariance of daily returns, annualised. (MOP use an exponentially weighted 60-day-centre-of-mass estimate; 63 days is the brief's simple version.)
- **Per-asset position:** x_i = s_i × 0.40 / σ_i (MOP's 40%/yr per asset). Raw portfolio: x_i / N.
- **Portfolio scaling:** multiply all raw weights by k = 0.10 / √(xᵀΣx/N²), the ex-ante vol of the raw portfolio, so the portfolio targets 10%/yr using only trailing data. Then cap gross exposure G = Σ|w_i| at **2.0** (Reg T: what a retail margin account can hold overnight): if G > 2, scale everything down to G = 2 (realised vol is then below 10%). Since k rescales everything, the 40% only matters through the 1/σ_i shape.

**★ LS (long-short, as published):** the above.

**★ LF (long-flat, retail):** the weights of the equal-risk benchmark (below) on that date, with each ETF whose signal is negative set to 0 and its weight left in BIL. The portfolio is not re-levered when assets go flat, so its risk falls when trends turn down (as in Part 2's Faber rule). LF's gross is therefore ≤ the benchmark's ≤ 2.

This LF construction is my choice. The alternative, re-scaling the remaining longs to 10%, would lever up a handful of assets whenever most trends are down, which isn't "flat".

## Benchmarks (same window, same cost model)

1. **Equal-risk long (the fair test):** the same 26 ETFs with s_i = +1 always, built exactly like LS (1/σ_i shape, scaled to 10% ex-ante vol, gross capped at 2), rebalanced monthly. Every config below gets its own matched benchmark (same assets, rebalance frequency and construction, signal always long).
2. 60/40 SPY/IEF, rebalanced monthly.
3. SPY buy and hold.

## Costs and financing

- **Trading:** 5 bp per side of traded value (primary), 15 bp (stress), as in Part 2; 0 bp printed for reference. Turnover includes the BIL (cash) leg, as in Part 2.
- **Accounting (retail margin account):** long dollar value L, short dollar value S (as positive), NAV = 1. Free cash F = 1 − L. If F ≥ 0 it's held in BIL. If F < 0, it's a margin loan costing BIL's return + 1.5%/yr. Short-sale proceeds are held as collateral and **earn nothing**; shorts pay a **1%/yr borrow fee** on S. So a short loses T-bill interest relative to a futures short, which is the real cost of shorting ETFs at a retail broker.
  - The brief says "leverage cost BIL + 1.5% on gross exposure above 100%". I apply it to the margin loan (longs above 100% of NAV), because a short doesn't need a loan; instead the short loses the T-bill interest on its proceeds, which for S ≤ 1 costs about the same or more. Stated here before running.
- **Cost cut, information only:** "futures-like" financing for LS, where short proceeds earn BIL. It isn't a config, just a reporting cut of the same positions.

## Measurements

For each config and its benchmarks, on daily returns from 2017-03-01 to 2026-11-13: CAGR, volatility, Sharpe (daily excess of BIL × √252, as Part 2), max drawdown, worst month, average gross, average |net|, share of months the gross cap binds, turnover a year, correlation to SPY (monthly returns), per-year returns, and returns over Feb–Mar 2020 (2020-01-31 close → 2020-03-31 close), calendar 2022, and April 2025 (2025-03-31 → 2025-04-30).

- **Alpha vs equal-risk benchmark:** OLS of monthly strategy excess return (calendar months, compounded from daily, minus BIL's month) on the benchmark's monthly excess; alpha × 12, beta, HC1 t. Same regression on SPY's excess, for information. Plus the monthly gap (strategy − benchmark) mean a year and t, with and without the 5 best months.
- **By asset class:** each class's contribution = Σ over days of w_{i,t−1} × (r_{i,t} − r_BIL,t), summed over its ETFs, annualised (arithmetic), for LS, LF and the benchmark. Also by ETF.
- Without 2020 and without 2022 (information).

**What t can ten years reach?** With T = 9.7 years, alpha t ≈ IR × √T = IR × 3.1, where IR is the appraisal ratio (alpha / residual vol). For a strategy with a Sharpe 0.3 above a benchmark (say 0.8 vs 0.5) and correlation ρ to it, IR = (0.8 − 0.5ρ)/√(1−ρ²): ρ = 0 → IR 0.8, t ≈ 2.5; ρ = 0.5 → 0.64, t ≈ 2.0; ρ = 0.8 → 0.67, t ≈ 2.1. A plain test of the Sharpe difference itself is weaker: its SE over 9.7 years is ~0.3–0.4, so a 0.3 gap has t ≈ 0.8–1. So the alpha bar of t ≥ 1.5 is reachable only if the Sharpe gap is ≥ ~0.25 and the strategy is not just a scaled copy of the benchmark. Failing it is plausible even if the effect is real at MOP's post-2009 strength (Sharpe ≈ 0.3–0.5).

## Pass rule (set in advance, separate verdict for LS and LF, each against its own equal-risk benchmark)

1. Sharpe > benchmark's Sharpe at 5 bp **and** at 15 bp.
2. Max drawdown smaller (less negative) than the benchmark's.
3. Calendar 2022 total return > 0 (the crisis-alpha prediction).
4. Alpha vs the equal-risk benchmark > 0 with HC1 t ≥ 1.5.

**PASS** if 1–4 all hold. **INCONCLUSIVE** ("behaves as published, not proven") if 1–3 hold and 4 doesn't. **FAIL** if any of 1–3 fails. Beating SPY or 60/40 isn't required (Part 2: this decade favoured US large caps); reported anyway. The robustness configs are information only and are judged by whether they agree in sign with ★.

- **The ETF implementation cost more than the edge.** LS needed about 0.95 of NAV in shorts and a gross of ~1.9. (Post hoc: the set has no long-USD ETF such as UUP, so LF's currency sleeve could only be long foreign currencies or flat.)
- **The benchmark itself is a poor portfolio** in this decade: Sharpe −0.14. It's 1/σ weighted, so SHY alone is ~0.48 of NAV, and it's levered to a gross of ~1.9 at BIL + 1.5%, in a decade when bonds and foreign currencies lost to T-bills. Beating it is a low bar, and LF only just cleared it.

### Conclusion

TSMOM's signal showed up in this decade at roughly its post-2009 strength. Before frictions it was about +2%/yr over T-bills (gross Sharpe ~0.3) above holding the same markets, it was uncorrelated with SPY, and it was positive in 2022 and flat in early 2020. But replicating it with ETFs in a retail margin account fails:
- **LS:** shorting ETFs forfeits the T-bill interest on the proceeds and pays borrow. Together with leverage and turnover, that's about 4%/yr, twice the gross edge.
- **LF:** avoids most of those costs and keeps Part 2's trend-following lesson: half the drawdown of its benchmark (−13% vs −25%) at a slightly better Sharpe. But its Sharpe of 0.10 is far below 60/40's, it lost money in 2022, and the alpha t is 1.13.

Both co-primaries fail the pre-registered rule. As in Part 2, what survives is drawdown control from going flat, not extra return. Diversifying from 5 to 26 markets didn't change that once costs and financing were counted.
